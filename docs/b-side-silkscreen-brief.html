Mixxtape B-Side

The back of the board is blank. It is the only surface on this object with no job to do, which makes it the one place the thing gets to say what it is.

Canvas 100.33 × 63.50 mm Process 1-colour silkscreen Ground matte white solder mask Existing art none
The object

A cassette you can actually record on

Mixxtape is a bare circuit board cut to the exact outline of a compact cassette — 100 × 63.5 mm — that slides into a real Norelco case. There is no enclosure and no bezel. The board is the product, so the artwork is not packaging for the object; it is the object's only finish.

A microphone on the front records three four-minute tracks. You hold REC to record and release to stop. It pairs to ordinary Bluetooth earbuds and plays back. Cassette-shaped Bluetooth players already exist; nobody ships a cassette-shaped recorder, and recording is the part that made a mixtape a mixtape.

The solder mask is matte white for a specific reason: glossy board finishes reject permanent marker, and this one is meant to be labelled by hand, the way you'd label a tape. Whatever goes on the back has to survive sharing the object with somebody's handwriting.

The canvas

What you are drawing on

The front face is fully committed — LEDs, four buttons, USB-C, the microphone, the radio module, and functional labels. The back has a solid copper ground plane under the mask and, right now, not a single mark on it.

Two things shape the composition before you draw anything. Two Ø14 mm holes pass clean through the board where a cassette's reel hubs would be, and a small tongue in one corner snaps off by hand — the write-protect tab, exactly like the one you'd break out of a real cassette to stop it being taped over.

Orientation

Draw it as you see it above — the view looking at the back of the board. Back-face artwork gets mirrored on its way into the manufacturing files, and that flip is handled on our side. If you design from the front-face coordinates instead, every asymmetric feature lands on the wrong side.

What the process can hold

Silkscreen is a stencil, not a print. There is one ink, fully opaque, with no tints and no halftones. Think in solid shapes and negative space; anything you would normally solve with a 40 % grey has to become a hatch, a stipple, or nothing.

  • Minimum line weight 0.15 mm. Thinner strokes break up or vanish. Assume 0.20 mm to be safe.
  • Minimum gap between marks 0.15 mm. Ink spreads; anything tighter fills in and reads as a blob.
  • Minimum text height 0.8 mm, and that is the floor, not a target. Below about 1.2 mm small type gets mushy on a matte surface.
  • Large solid fills are allowed and print well, but they show every via dimple and every fingerprint. A big black field is a real option, just an opinionated one.
Measured against the current artwork

The peony design now on the board has 32 shapes thinner than 0.15 mm in one dimension, and 52 more between 0.15 and 0.25 mm. The 32 will not survive the screen — they will fill in or drop out. The marginal 52 are a coin toss on a matte surface. Thickening the finest stems and closing the smallest gaps is the one change that will most improve how the printed board actually looks.

Existing language

What the front already says

The front is purely functional and set in plain uppercase at 2 mm: WRITE, REC, PLAY, TRACK, MODE, MIC, CPU, FLASH, POWER. It reads like test equipment, because that is what it is.

The back is not obliged to match that. Treating the two faces as a deliberate pair — instrument on the front, object on the back — is a legitimate and probably better answer than continuity for its own sake. That is your call to make and argue.

The ask

What the artwork has to carry

Only two things are genuinely required. Everything else is yours.

  1. The name and a revision. Board revisions get spun often and a board you cannot identify in a drawer is a nuisance. Design the lockup so the revision can change without redrawing anything.
  2. A project URL, so someone who finds one of these in eight years can work out what it is.

Beyond that, the open ground is the cassette metaphor itself — the hub teeth, the tape path, the A-side/B-side conceit, the ruled write-on label, the typography of a 1980s tape shell. The two reel windows are already the strongest shapes on the face; a composition that ignores them will fight them.

Open questions

Decide these before you start

What is it called? “Mixxtape” is a working title and is expected to change. Do not build a lockup that only works with this exact word.
What colour is the ink? — settled Black, and there is no choice in it. JLCPCB prints black silkscreen on white solder mask as a fixed pairing. White mask also adds about two days to the lead time.
Does the back get a write-on area too? The front carries a ruled label block for a sharpie. A real cassette labels both sides. Worth deciding rather than defaulting.
Under what licence? The repository has no licence file yet. If the artwork ships with an open-hardware project, its terms need settling up front.
Any marks required? The radio is a pre-certified module, but the finished product may still need identifiers on it. Leave a small reserved area until this is confirmed.
Delivery

How to hand it back

  • Vector, 1:1, in millimetres. SVG or DXF. The canvas must be exactly 100.33 × 63.50 mm so it drops straight onto the board.
  • Monochrome, single layer. One colour, pure black on transparent. No groups relying on opacity, blend modes, or clipping to look right — those do not survive the conversion.
  • All type converted to outlines, and all strokes expanded to filled shapes. What the fab receives is a region, not a path with a weight.
  • Drawn in the back-view orientation shown above, matching the template.
One thing to check before you draw

Open the template and confirm your tool reads the board as 100.33 mm wide. Illustrator and Figma both have opinions about SVG units, and a file that comes back at 96 DPI instead of millimetres is the single most common way this goes wrong.
